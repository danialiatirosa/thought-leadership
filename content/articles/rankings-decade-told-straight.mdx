<Finding index={5} num="2024" title="In 2024, both publishers rewrote the rules">
Times Higher Education replaced its long-standing Citations score with two new categories (Research Environment and Research Quality). QS added three new factors (Sustainability, Employment Outcomes, International Research Network) and increased the weight of Employer Reputation. Any rank change between 2023 and 2024 should be read as a rule change first, and an institutional change second.
</Finding>

<Finding index={6} num=">100" unit="places" title="QS and THE often disagree by 100 places or more, and that's the system working, not breaking">
For dozens of universities in 2026, the QS rank and the Times Higher Education rank are more than 100 places apart, and that gap is structural rather than random. QS rewards reputation; THE rewards research output. A university that looks elite on one list and middling on the other is being measured by two different yardsticks. The right response to a 100-place gap is to look at which factors drove it, not to dismiss either rank.
</Finding>

<p style={{ marginTop: 24 }}>
Four countries account for nearly all of the movement below the very top. Three of them are growth stories built on sustained funding; the fourth is where the erosion is concentrated.
</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>China added 24 universities to the Times Higher Education Top 500</strong> across the decade, and tripled its Top 100 cohort from 4 to 12. Six universities account for most of the elite-tier gain: Tsinghua, Peking, Fudan, Shanghai Jiao Tong, Zhejiang, and the University of Science and Technology of China.</li>
  <li><strong>Saudi Arabia went from one university in the Top 500 to nine.</strong> Vision 2030 named an explicit target ("at least five institutions in the global Top 200") and turned rank movement into a ministerial KPI. The Top-500 cohort grew accordingly, from one institution to nine, though the Top-200 line has not yet been crossed at scale.</li>
  <li><strong>The UAE went from zero universities in the Times Higher Education Top 500 to seven.</strong> The fastest percentage growth of any Gulf country. Khalifa University did most of the work; details in Section 03.</li>
  <li><strong>The United States is still the largest single-country cohort in both rankings.</strong> It lost ground, but the lost ground is in the 200-to-500 band, not at the top. Harvard, MIT and Stanford are exactly where they were. Princeton drifted a few places on QS but holds its top-five position on Times Higher Education. The mid-tier public flagships are where the erosion is.</li>
</ul>

<p style={{ marginTop: 32 }}>
Khalifa moved from the QS 401-410 band in 2017 to integer rank 177 in 2026, a roughly 228-place gain and the largest decade-long rise of any Gulf university in either ranking. On Times Higher Education, Khalifa entered the rankings in 2018 in the 301-350 band and now sits in the 201-250 band, just outside the integer-ranked tier. Abu Dhabi University moved faster still on Times Higher Education: entering the Top 500 in 2023 already in the 301-350 band, it reached an outright rank of 191 by 2025 (the only UAE institution to escape THE's banding entirely this decade) before settling back into the 201-250 tier alongside Khalifa in 2026.
</p>

<p>
The structural explanation is the 2017 consolidation that brought Khalifa University, the Petroleum Institute and the Masdar Institute together into a single research-intensive flagship. The pattern in the underlying scores is what makes the case instructive: <strong>Khalifa's research-output scores moved more than its reputation scores. The gain came from doing more research, not from being better known for it, and reputation typically catches up a few years later.</strong>
</p>

<p>
Abu Dhabi University's rise runs on a different engine. Chancellor Ghassan Aouad took over in January 2023 (the same year the university first entered the Times Higher Education Top 500) and has since pushed research output, citations and industry engagement broadly rather than through a single merger. Abu Dhabi University now ranks first in the UAE for citations per paper, and its International Outlook score, built on international faculty and student recruitment, ranks 13th globally. It is a second UAE institution running a research-led strategy, reached without Khalifa's consolidation story.
</p>

<p>
The model is what makes the case worth attention. The UAE merged two specialist institutions, the Petroleum Institute and the Masdar Institute, into Khalifa University, then funded the consolidated flagship as a Vision 2071 priority. This is the Singapore approach (one or two world-class flagships, sustained over decades) at a one-flagship scale.
</p>

<p>
Saudi Arabia took a different route to a bigger outcome. Vision 2030 set an explicit, time-bound target ("at least five institutions in the global Top 200") and funded a broader base of universities rather than a single flagship: nine Saudi institutions now sit in the Times Higher Education Top 500, up from one in 2016, and five in the QS Top 500, up from three in 2017. King Fahd University of Petroleum and Minerals leads the cohort at QS 67, but the growth is spread across public universities in Riyadh, Jeddah and the Eastern Province, not concentrated in one campus.
</p>

<p>
That broad base isn't uniformly durable, though, and King Abdulaziz University is the cautionary case inside it. King Abdulaziz reached an outright Times Higher Education rank of 101 in 2023 (within reach of the global Top 100), then fell to the 251-300 band in 2024 and the 351-400 band by 2025, where it remains in 2026. Part of that rise was built on paying prominent international researchers to add King Abdulaziz as a secondary affiliation, a practice reported on Saudi universities generally since the early 2010s, which inflated highly-cited-researcher counts and citation-impact scores without a matching gain in research capacity on the ground. When Times Higher Education cut the weight of raw citation impact from 30 percent to 15 percent of the overall score in its 2024 methodology overhaul (see Section 04, Inside the scores, for the fuller rule change), King Abdulaziz's rank fell with it. King Fahd's climb held up across the same rule change. A rank built on research and industry ties survives a methodology rewrite; a rank built on citation engineering does not.
</p>

<p>
Qatar's footprint tells the opposite story: small, and largely unchanged, though two structural factors flatter the comparison rather less than they should. Qatar's population is under three million, the smallest base of any GCC system in this report by a wide margin, and its most visible higher-education investment doesn't register in a country count at all: the Education City branch campuses of Georgetown, Northwestern, Cornell, Texas A&M<sup>†</sup>, Carnegie Mellon, VCU and HEC Paris are ranked under their home institutions, not as Qatari entries. What the count actually captures is the two homegrown universities: Qatar University has held a single Times Higher Education Top 500 place since 2018 (zero in 2016), and Qatar's QS count moved from one institution to two as Hamad Bin Khalifa University joined the list at rank 244. Qatar's bet was neither the UAE's single-flagship consolidation nor Saudi Arabia's broad-base funding, but importing prestige through branch campuses rather than building it into a rankable local one. That's a strategy this decade's rankings data structurally cannot see.
</p>

<p>
Khalifa moved faster than the Saudi cohort because it was built on existing research infrastructure; consolidation always compounds faster than spreading a similar amount of funding across many institutions. The Saudi cohort grew larger in absolute count because the country was working from a wider starting base of universities. <strong>Both approaches are valid. The choice depends on what timeline a funder is willing to hold, and how many institutions a system already has to build from.</strong>
</p>

<PullQuote cite="On the Gulf's three ranking strategies">
Consolidation, broad-base funding, and citation engineering all move a rank. Only the first two survive a methodology rewrite.
</PullQuote>

<p style={{ marginTop: 32 }}>
Two goals matter from here for the UAE: get more of its universities into the rankings, and push the ones already in further up them. The Khalifa case, and the Gulf comparison above, point to different levers for each stakeholder in that system; generalised, those levers run through the six-agenda framework in Section 06, Lessons & Implications.
</p>

<p style={{ marginTop: 8, marginBottom: 8 }}><strong>Two mechanisms explain almost every rank-gap surprise.</strong></p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Reputation lags reality, on both sides.</strong> QS asks tens of thousands of academics and employers what they think of universities, 45% of its score. THE runs its own academic reputation survey too, folded into the Teaching and Research Environment pillars for 33% of its score. Either way, reputation views update slowly: a university that improved five years ago is still being judged on its older work, on either list. A university that slipped five years ago still benefits from its old reputation. The lag runs in both directions, and it is long.</li>
  <li><strong>Research output moves faster.</strong> A university that publishes more, and is cited more, climbs the research-output side of the rankings in three to five years. Times Higher Education rewards this more than QS does (30% of the THE score sits in purely bibliometric Research Quality indicators, against 20% in QS's Citations per Faculty).</li>
  <li><strong>Together, they sort universities into two types.</strong> A Chinese research university that rose on citation output, like USTC (QS 132, THE 51), typically ranks higher on Times Higher Education: the research-speed mechanism, since 30% of THE's score sits in a research-quality pillar that rewards bibliometric output more directly than QS's structure does. A long-established American university with a deep reputation footprint, like Cornell (QS 16, THE 18), typically ranks higher on QS: the reputation-lag mechanism, since 45% of QS's score is two reputation surveys, a weight THE's equivalent surveys (33% combined) don't match. The same two mechanisms explain wider gaps too. King Abdulaziz University (QS 163, THE 351–400 band) is a reputation-and-citations story taken further: strong enough on the indicators QS weights most (its two reputation surveys plus citations per faculty, 65% of the QS score combined) to rank well there, without a matching standing on THE's differently-normalised research-quality and teaching pillars. University of California, Santa Cruz (QS 458, THE 181) runs the opposite way: a public research campus with a research profile that shows up well across THE's broader pillar structure, but without the global reputation-survey visibility or the strong faculty-to-student ratio that QS's heavier 55% reputation-plus-ratio weighting rewards. Neither ranking is wrong; they are weighing different things.</li>
  <li><strong>Use this to read any 100-place gap.</strong> A university that looks like a star on one list and middling on the other is almost never a fluke. Check which of the two patterns above it matches; that tells you what kind of university it is.</li>
</ul>

  title="Three forces will reshape the league tables before 2030."
  italic="reshape"
  dark

<p className="lead">
The decade's clearest lesson is that rankings move slowest at the very top, fastest at the bottom of the Top 500, and most discontinuously when the publishers change their rules. The next five years will be shaped by three forces, none of them in the universities' direct control.
</p>

<h3>AI-augmented research is the single largest variable.</h3>
<p>
Universities that institutionalise AI tools in their research operations (for literature review, hypothesis generation, experimental design, and analysis) will see their citation scores compound faster than universities that do not. The differential will not be uniform. It will favour large, integrated research universities with the data infrastructure to operationalise AI at scale, and it will work against smaller specialist institutions that lack the supporting systems. Expect a sharper separation between the global Top 30 and the rest of the Top 100 by 2030.
</p>

<h3>Geopolitical fragmentation is a real and growing risk.</h3>
<p>
The post-2022 decoupling between Russia and Western research networks has already produced the only major negative country-level shift in our citation analysis. A similar but slower decoupling between the United States and China would have larger effects, because the citation networks involved are larger. Cross-border research collaboration is a tailwind for many of the universities in the current Top 100; if it becomes a constraint, the geographic distribution of the elite tier will narrow. Neutral-ground research hosts, including the UAE, would become relative beneficiaries.
</p>

<h3>Demographics will tighten Western higher-education margins.</h3>
<p>
The shrinking 18-year-old populations of North America, Western Europe, and East Asia (notably South Korea and Japan) will tighten margins at universities that depend on tuition revenue. The demographic dividend in India, Southeast Asia, and Sub-Saharan Africa is real but will not produce new Top-500 universities for at least 15 years. New universities take that long to build the research depth, reputation footprint, and faculty base the rankings require. The compositional shifts of the next decade will continue to flow through existing institutions, not new ones.
</p>

<p style={{ marginTop: 32 }}>
The clearest planning fact for the next five years is that another rule change is plausible, and probably coming. The 2024 rewrites were not the end of the publishers' adjustments. <strong>Any university or funder making a five-year ranking strategy that does not include "the rules may change again" as a planning assumption is making a fragile bet.</strong>
</p>

<SectionHeader
  number="08"
  kicker="Methodology & sources"
  title="How we built the dataset."
/>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Coverage.</strong> QS Top 500, 2017–2026 (ten editions). THE Top 500, 2016–2026 (eleven editions). 1,038 distinct canonical institutions across 96 countries; 10,536 institution-year-system rows.</li>
  <li><strong>Rank handling.</strong> Integer ranks pass through unchanged. Banded ranks (e.g., "401–500") converted to numerical midpoints with the original band retained for tier-boundary analysis. Tie markers stripped.</li>
  <li><strong>Country normalisation.</strong> ISO-3166 alpha-3 via pycountry, plus manual aliases for Hong Kong, Macao, Taiwan, Iran, Korea, Russia, the UAE, and other commonly inconsistent entries. Greater China (China + Hong Kong + Macao + Taiwan) retained as a separate analytical view.</li>
  <li><strong>Institution matching.</strong> Three rules in order: hard alias for known cases; exact match on aggressively-normalised name; controlled fuzzy match (token_sort_ratio ≥ 97 AND first significant token agrees AND length ratio ≥ 0.75). All fuzzy edges audited.</li>
  <li><strong>2024 rule-change flagging.</strong> Year-over-year rank changes computed only for integer-ranked rows. The 2024 changes (THE WUR 3.0; QS revision) are flagged in the data; cross-year analyses straddling the change are discussed as a rule change first throughout. Validated against a 28-institution truth panel with anti-collision tests.</li>
</ul>
