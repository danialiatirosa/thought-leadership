Since these rankings began in 2004, institutions in North America and Europe have dominated both tables. In 2016 they held 85 places in Times Higher Education's Top 100; in 2027 they hold 73. The Gulf, starting from a negligible base, has emerged as a second source of growth. In 2024 both QS and THE rewrote their methods, which shuffled the tables more than anything the universities themselves did."

<Finding index={7} num="2024" title="In 2024, both publishers rewrote the rules">
THE added three research-quality measures (Research Strength, Excellence and Influence) alongside its citation score; QS added Sustainability, Employment Outcomes and International Research Network, and gave employer views more weight. The typical university moved 15 places on THE and 23 on QS, far more than in any other year of the decade. The top barely changed, but lower down some universities swung by more than 100 places, including King Abdulaziz University on THE and four Russian universities on QS. Read any rank change between 2023 and 2024 as a rule change first, and an institutional change second.
</Finding>

<Finding index={8} num=">100" unit="places" title="QS and THE often disagree by 100 places or more">
For dozens of universities in 2027, the QS rank and the THE rank sit more than 100 places apart. QS rewards reputation. THE rewards research output. A university that looks elite on one list and average on the other is being measured by two different frameworks.
</Finding>

In September 2026 an AI model reportedly proved a result on the Navier-Stokes equations, an early sign that AI can produce real research on its own.

<p style={{ marginTop: 24 }}>
<strong>Four countries account for nearly all the movement below the very top.</strong> Three are growth stories built on sustained funding; the US is where the erosion sits.
</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>China added 25 universities to the THE WUR Top 500 across the decade</strong>, and quadrupled its Top 100 cohort, from 2 to 8. China's climb traces back to one policy decision: the Double First-Class programme. It was announced in 2015 to replace the older Project 985 and Project 211<sup>†</sup>. It funds roughly 140 universities and disciplines to build "world-class" research capacity, in sustained five-year funding cycles from 2016. Most of the elite-tier gain came from institutions that were already large and established: Tsinghua, Peking, Fudan, Shanghai Jiao Tong, Zhejiang, and the University of Science and Technology of China. The funding built on existing strength. However, the funding isn't unconditional. Each five-year cycle includes a review mechanism. After the first one, the Ministry of Education publicly warned or revoked 16 underperforming disciplines across 15 universities.</li>
  <li><strong>Saudi Arabia went from one WUR Top 500 university to nine.</strong> Vision 2030 set a clear target, at least five in the global Top 200, and made rank movement a ministerial KPI. Its five highest-ranked universities on QS are King Fahd University of Petroleum and Minerals (KFUPM, 63), King Saud University (107), King Abdulaziz University (200), Prince Mohammad Bin Fahd University (421) and Imam Abdulrahman Bin Faisal University (474), so three already meet the bar. On THE, only KFUPM (176) is in the Top 200.</li>
  <li><strong>The UAE went from zero WUR Top 500 universities to six</strong>, the second-largest Gulf gain after Saudi Arabia. On THE they are Khalifa University and United Arab Emirates University (both 235), Abu Dhabi University and the University of Sharjah (both 301–325), Ajman University (376–400) and Al Ain University (401–450).</li>
  <li><strong>The US is still the largest single-country cohort in both rankings, but its number of universities in THE's Top 500 fell from 122 to 96 between 2016 and 2027.</strong> Most of those that dropped out are public universities that were not in the Top 100. Harvard, MIT and Stanford have barely moved. Even among the elite privates there are some exceptions: Princeton drifted from 11th to 27th on QS while climbing to 3rd on THE, and Brown and Dartmouth fell on QS, from 49th to 66th and from 158th to 270th.</li>
</ul>


<p className="no-indent" style={{ marginTop: 24 }}>
<strong>The ranking tables themselves have grown over the last decade.</strong> Times Higher Education ranked 980 universities in its 2017 edition and 2,297 in 2027; QS's table grew from 916 to 1,504. Most of this growth has come further down the tables: between its 2020 and 2026 editions, 919 universities joined THE's table and only 45 of them were ranked in the Top 500, while 124 withdrew. Some withdrawals were protests against the rankings themselves. Seven Indian Institutes of Technology, including Bombay, Delhi and Madras, announced in 2020 that they would not take part, citing a lack of transparency in THE's methods, and Utrecht University left in 2023, arguing that a single score cannot capture a university's work.
</p>

<p style={{ marginTop: 32 }}>
<strong>Singapore is the counter-example: it barely grew its footprint.</strong> Its THE count held at exactly two institutions, NUS and NTU, for ten years<sup>‡</sup>, until the Singapore University of Technology and Design joined in 2027; neither NUS nor NTU appears on the risers list, because both were already elite in 2017. What moved is how elite: NUS climbed from QS 12th to 10th, and NTU's bigger move was on THE, from 54th to 28th. NUS also remains Asia's highest-ranked university on QS. Below it, two smaller universities climbed sharply in QS's 2027 edition: SUTD from 519th to 266th, and Singapore Management University from 511th to 411th.
</p>

<p style={{ marginTop: 32 }}>
<strong>The biggest risers are linked to robust national funding programmes.</strong>
</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Seven of the 15 risers are mainland Chinese universities</strong>, all funded under the Double First-Class programme.</li>
  <li><strong>Two more risers are Hong Kong universities</strong> (Hong Kong Polytechnic and the Chinese University of Hong Kong), funded by Hong Kong's University Grants Committee.</li>
  <li><strong>Four are Korean universities funded by Brain Korea 21</strong> (Yonsei, KAIST, Sungkyunkwan and Korea University), a graduate-research programme running since 1999. Korea's climb is two and a half decades of steady investment.</li>
  <li><strong>The other two are Germany's Charité</strong>, a specialist medical university funded by the Excellence Strategy, <strong>and Italy's University of Bologna</strong>.</li>
</ul>

<p style={{ marginTop: 32 }}>
<strong>Not all of these risers are STEM specialists.</strong> A few are technical institutes or medical universities, but most are comprehensive research universities. Fudan, for instance, is known for the humanities and social sciences, not as a STEM specialist. The funding behind these climbs targets research output broadly, not STEM alone.
</p>


<p style={{ marginTop: 32 }}>
One pattern holds across the entire risers list. <strong>The universities that climbed are the ones whose governments committed to sustained funding, paired with strong accountability mechanisms.</strong> The gains came with a lag: China named its first funded universities in 2017, and its biggest climbs followed from 2019 onwards. National commitment eventually shows up in the rankings, regardless of region; how quickly depends on how much infrastructure the system already had.
</p>

<h3 style={{ display: 'flex', alignItems: 'center', gap: 10, marginTop: 40 }}><Flag country="United Arab Emirates" width={24} /> United Arab Emirates</h3>

<p>
<strong>Khalifa University is the UAE's biggest riser.</strong> Its QS rank moved from the 401-410 band in 2017<sup>‡</sup> to 147 in 2027. That's a roughly 258-place gain, the largest of any UAE university this decade, and second in the Gulf only to Qatar University, which climbed 284 places, from 393rd in 2017 to 109th in 2027. On THE, it moved from the 501-600 band in 2017 to rank 235 in 2027. Khalifa's research-output scores are well ahead of its reputation scores: it ranks 87th in the world on QS's citations per faculty, but outside the top 300 on both reputation surveys. Reputation usually catches up a few years later. In 2017, Khalifa University merged with the Petroleum Institute and the Masdar Institute by presidential decree, creating a single flagship. It's the same approach Singapore used, one or two strong universities, funded for decades.
</p>

<p>
<strong>The intent behind the climb is visible in how Khalifa is run.</strong> Its board is appointed by Abu Dhabi Executive Council resolution and composed largely of executives from Mubadala, ADNOC, ADQ, ADIA and the UAE Space Agency rather than academics. The university attributes its rise to strategic investment in research, talent and global partnerships, and its hiring reflects this: it ranks 12th in the world on QS's international faculty measure. When Khalifa entered the QS Top 150 in the 2027 edition, President Ebrahim Al Hajri said it "reflects a deliberate choice to ensure the discoveries made in our laboratories reach scientists, industries, and institutions around the world."
</p>

<p>
<strong>Four of Abu Dhabi's government-backed universities do not appear in either ranking as UAE entries.</strong> NYU Abu Dhabi, IIT Delhi–Abu Dhabi and Sorbonne University Abu Dhabi are satellite campuses of New York University (QS 58, THE 29), IIT Delhi (QS 118; not in THE's table) and Sorbonne University in Paris (QS 73; withdrew from THE's rankings from the 2027 edition), and are not listed separately. MBZUAI is in neither table: both require undergraduate teaching, and its first undergraduates only arrived in 2025.
</p>


<h3 style={{ display: 'flex', alignItems: 'center', gap: 10 }}><Flag country="Saudi Arabia" width={24} /> Saudi Arabia</h3>

<p>
<strong>Saudi Arabia spread its funding across a broad base of public universities in Riyadh, Jeddah and the Eastern Province.</strong> Vision 2030 set a clear target: at least five institutions in the global Top 200. The THE WUR Top 500 count went from 1 to 9. The QS count went from 3 to 6.
</p>

<p>
<strong>King Fahd University of Petroleum and Minerals (KFUPM) leads the group at QS 63, the first Arab university to break into the QS Top 100.</strong> It became independent from the government in 2020. Its president, Dr. Muhammad Al-Saggaf, came the same year from KAPSARC. He built 20 new research centres, added 43 undergraduate concentrations, and grew the postdoc base roughly tenfold towards a target of 1,500. The rank barely moved until 2023, then jumped from 160 to 101 to 63 by 2027.
</p>

<p>
<strong>KAUST doesn't appear in either ranking</strong>: it's graduate-only, and both systems require undergraduate teaching to qualify. It has topped THE's Arab University Rankings three years running instead, in the 2024 to 2026 editions. KFUPM leads the same QS regional table.
</p>

<p>
<strong>King Abdulaziz University is the warning sign inside Saudi Arabia's broad base.</strong> It hit an outright THE rank of 101 in 2023, then fell to the 351-400 band by 2025, and to 401–450 in 2027. Part of that rise came from incentivizing international researchers to list it as a secondary affiliation<sup>§</sup>. That inflated its citation counts without adding real research capacity. When THE cut the weight of citations from 30 percent to 15 percent in its 2024 overhaul, the rank fell with it. KFUPM's climb held. <strong>A rank built on research capability and industry ties survives a methodology change. One built on citation engineering doesn't.</strong>
</p>

<p style={{ marginTop: 8, marginBottom: 8 }}><strong>Two mechanisms explain almost every rank-gap surprise.</strong></p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Reputation lags reality, on both sides.</strong> QS asks tens of thousands of academics and employers what they think of universities, which accounts for 45% of its score. THE runs its own academic teaching and research reputation survey too, for 33% of its score. Reputation views update slowly. A university that improved five years ago is still being judged on its older work. A university that slipped five years ago still benefits from its old reputation.</li>
  <li><strong>Research output moves faster.</strong> A university that publishes more, and is cited more, climbs the research-output side of the rankings in three to five years. THE rewards this more than QS does. 30% of the THE score sits in purely bibliometric Research Quality indicators. QS's Citations per Faculty is only 20%.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>These mechanisms sort universities into two types.</strong> Take the University of Science and Technology of China (QS 134, THE 51). It rose on citation output, so it typically ranks higher on THE. That's the research-speed mechanism: THE weights research quality 30% against QS's 20% for citations. Now take Cornell (QS 16, THE 21), a long-established American university with a deep reputation footprint. It typically ranks higher on QS. That's the reputation-lag mechanism: QS weights reputation 45% against THE's 33%.</p>

<p>The same two mechanisms explain wider gaps too. University of California, Santa Cruz (QS 458, THE 201) is a public research campus with a profile that shows up well across THE's research metrics. But it lacks the global reputation-survey visibility and the strong faculty-to-student ratio that QS's heavier 55% reputation-plus-ratio weighting rewards. Neither ranking is wrong. They're weighing different things.</p>

<strong>Between 2023 and 2024, universities moved on the rankings about three times as much as they did in any other year of the decade.</strong> That is too big to come from anything the universities themselves did. Almost all of it is the rule change.
</p>

<p style={{ marginTop: 32 }}>
<strong>A score that once secured a top-100 place no longer does.</strong> Both publishers convert each university's results into scores relative to the other universities ranked that year, so a score shows where a university stands in that edition rather than a fixed level of performance. On THE, the score at 100th place rose steadily from 58.0 in 2016 to 63.4 in 2023, before any rule change, then jumped to 66.7 with THE's 2024 rewrite; it stood at 66.6 in 2027. On QS, it stayed between 58 and 65 from 2017 to 2025, then rose to 68.5 in 2026. A university can therefore keep the same score and still slip down the table, without its own performance having declined.

<ArticleSection id="outlook" variant="dark">

<SectionHeader
  number="05"
  kicker="The next decade"
  title="Five forces will reshape the rankings."
  italic="reshape"
  dark
/>

<p className="lead">
This decade's clearest lesson: rankings move slowest at the very top. They move fastest at the bottom of the WUR Top 500. Five forces will shape the next decade.
</p>

<h3>AI-augmented research is the biggest variable.</h3>
<p>
AI adoption in research is already large and undisclosed. <strong>A 2025 study found that about 14% of PubMed biomedical abstracts from 2024 show signs of large-language-model writing.</strong><sup>†</sup> Disclosure lags far behind actual use. Universities that build AI into research work will see citation scores rise faster than those that don't. Large research universities with strong data systems will benefit most. Generative AI makes it cheap to mass-produce low-quality research papers. Journals now catch and retract about ten times as many research papers as they did a decade ago.
</p>

<p style={{ marginTop: 24 }}>
So far, AI is reviewing and speeding up research humans still do. The bigger shift is AI producing research on its own. In September 2026, OpenAI said its model proved that the three-dimensional Navier-Stokes equations, which describe how fluids flow, can develop a singularity in finite time. That question had been open for centuries. Days earlier, two mathematicians using Anthropic's models claimed a related result on the simpler Euler equations. Neither claim has been peer-reviewed yet. This shows AI now taking on problems that have resisted mathematicians for generations.
</p>

<h3>Geopolitical fragmentation is a growing risk.</h3>
<p>
<strong>Russia's presence in both rankings has fallen sharply: it had seven universities in THE's Top 500 in 2016 and has one in 2027, and its QS count fell from 17 in 2023 to seven in 2027.</strong> That reflects its isolation from Western research networks since 2022. A similar decoupling between the US and China would matter more, as the citation networks are bigger. Cross-border research collaboration helps many WUR Top 100 universities today. If it shrinks, the elite tier will come from fewer countries. Neutral hosts, including the UAE, would benefit.
</p>

<h3>Demographics will tighten Western higher-education margins.</h3>
<p>
<strong>18-year-old populations are shrinking across North America, Western Europe, and East Asia</strong>, especially in South Korea and Japan. That will tighten margins at tuition-dependent universities. India, Southeast Asia, and Sub-Saharan Africa have growing young populations. But that won't produce new WUR Top-500 universities for at least 15 years. New universities need time to build research depth, reputation, and faculty. The next decade's changes will run through existing institutions, not new ones.
</p>

<h3>The single "world ranking" is splitting into many rankings.</h3>
<p>
QS and THE already run regional and specialist rankings alongside their flagship World University Rankings. Each has different eligibility rules and winners. KAUST can't appear in either flagship table as they require an undergraduate programme, and KAUST doesn't have one. But KAUST has topped THE's Arab University Rankings for three straight years, in the 2024 to 2026 editions. King Fahd University of Petroleum and Minerals, not KAUST, leads the same QS regional table. <strong>A university's rank depends on which list it participates in. By 2030, "how is this university ranked" will need a follow-up: which ranking, measuring what.</strong>
</p>

<h3>Publishers will keep rewriting the rules.</h3>
<p>
<strong>Neither publisher has gone long without a major methodology change.</strong> QS added Sustainability and International Research Network in 2023, then International Student Diversity in 2025. THE shipped its WUR 3.0 rewrite in 2024. It responded to the same citation-engineering this report documents at King Abdulaziz University in Section 03. AI now makes that kind of gaming cheaper and easier to scale. <strong>Our best guess: the next rule change will target research-integrity screening directly.</strong> It would catch retracted-paper citations, AI-generated output, or undisclosed AI use before they inflate a score, instead of years later through retractions. <strong>Research integrity, not teaching quality or reputation, is the most exposed part of either methodology today.</strong>
</p>

<ArticleSection id="implications" variant="alt">

<SectionHeader
  number="06"
  kicker="Implications"
  title="Fund with intent."
/>

<p className="lead">
This decade's data speaks most directly to governments and sovereign funders. They decide where the money goes. What follows rests on mechanisms covered elsewhere in this report: reputation lag, funding-to-climb lag, and how much a system concentrates effort instead of spreading it thin.
</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Be clear about what you want from rankings before you fund for them.</strong> Rankings reward research quality, internationalisation, and employment outcomes. QS weights reputation and research output most; THE leans harder into research quality and teaching. They don't measure teaching quality, social mobility, or regional impact. Fund institutions to climb the rankings, and you're funding what rankings measure, not institutional quality as a whole.</li>
  <li><strong>Fund a selective number of fields or institutions on ten-year cycles, not three-year budgets.</strong> Fund what rankings actually reward: research output, citations, international research networks, and employment outcomes, not names on buildings. These are the specific, measurable inputs behind every score. A building doesn't move a citations-per-faculty number. A decade of sustained faculty hiring in a chosen field does.</li>
  <li><strong>Set a numbered, time-bound target, and report against it annually.</strong> Saudi Arabia did this: "at least five institutions in the Top 200 by 2030" focused ministerial effort and created accountability. It paid off early. King Fahd University of Petroleum and Minerals became the first Arab university to break into the QS Top 100, years ahead of schedule.</li>
  <li><strong>Building from scratch and funding an existing university run on different timelines. Know which one you're signing up for.</strong> Building a new university from nothing (KAUST, MBZUAI) takes more than 15 years to reach Top-200 standing. Funding an existing university harder (China's Double First-Class, Khalifa after 2017) shows up in five to seven years. Both work. Changing strategy after three years, when a new minister or board arrives, is a common, costly mistake. Patience doesn't mean no oversight: Double First-Class pairs its long cycles with real accountability, publicly warning or revoking underperforming disciplines at each five-year review.</li>
  <li><strong>Treat international-student visa policy as research policy.</strong> International graduates who stay co-author papers that compound into citation scores over five to ten years. Both major ranking systems reward this: it feeds QS's international-network indicators and THE's International Outlook pillar. The UAE Golden Visa, the UK Graduate Route, the German Blue Card, and the Singapore TechPass all function as citation-score instruments. That's exactly the kind of open channel geopolitical fragmentation would close first.</li>
  <li><strong>A hosted branch campus almost never earns a rank of its own. Judge it by what it does for your other universities, not by a climb it can't make.</strong> Its researchers co-author with your home-grown institutions. That lifts those universities' international-network and co-authorship scores, whether or not the branch campus itself ever appears in a league table. Qatar's Education City, which hosts branch campuses of Georgetown, Northwestern, Cornell, and others, is the clearest example. None of those campuses shows up as a separate Qatari entry in either ranking. Yet the model still does real work for the country's research profile.</li>
</ul>

<p style={{ marginTop: 32 }}>
None of this changes what already happened. It only changes what to do next.
</p>
