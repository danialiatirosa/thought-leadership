From 2 institutions in 2016 to 7 in 2026. This is the single biggest change in the elite tier of either ranking. <Finding index={3} num="−4 / −20" tone="neg" trend="down" title="The US lost ground at the WUR Top 100, and far more sharply below it">
In the WUR Top 100, the decline is real but mild: 39 to 35 (THE) and 32 to 26 (QS), concentrated in mid-Top-100 publics, not Ivy-tier privates. The very top barely moves at all: the median annual rank shift for Top-10 institutions is just one place on either system, and Harvard, MIT and Stanford hold almost exactly the ranks they held a decade ago. The US still holds the largest single-country cohort in both systems. In the WUR Top 500, a cohort roughly five times larger, the erosion is sharper: a net loss of 20 institutions, the decade's largest net decline of any country (see chart).

<Finding index={5} num="2024" title="In 2024, both publishers rewrote the rules">
Times Higher Education replaced its long-standing Citations score with two new categories (Research Environment and Research Quality). QS added three new factors (Sustainability, Employment Outcomes, International Research Network) and increased the weight of Employer Reputation. Any rank change between 2023 and 2024 should be read as a rule change first, and an institutional change second.
</Finding>

<Finding index={6} num=">100" unit="places" title="QS and THE often disagree by 100 places or more, and that's the system working, not breaking">
For dozens of universities in 2026, the QS rank and the Times Higher Education rank are more than 100 places apart, and that gap is structural rather than random. QS rewards reputation; THE rewards research output. A university that looks elite on one list and middling on the other is being measured by two different yardsticks. The right response to a 100-place gap is to look at which factors drove it, not to dismiss either rank.
</Finding>

<Finding index={7} num="14%" trend="up" title="AI is already reshaping how research gets done, not just how it's written up">
That's the share of 2024 PubMed biomedical abstracts already showing textual signatures of large-language-model writing<sup>†</sup>, and disclosure lags far behind actual use. AI is no longer only assisting research, either: a September 2026 claim of a machine-generated proof on a Millennium Prize-level maths problem is an early sign of AI starting to produce genuine research on its own. Expect AI-augmented research to be the single largest variable behind who moves in the rankings before 2030 (Section 05, Outlook to 2030).
</Finding>

<p style={{ marginTop: 32 }}>
Singapore is the counter-example to every story above: it didn't grow its footprint at all, and that was the point. Its Times Higher Education count has held at exactly two institutions, National University of Singapore and Nanyang Technological University, for the entire decade<sup>†</sup>; neither appears on the risers list, because both were already inside the global elite in 2017. What moved is how elite: NUS climbed from QS 12th to 8th and Times Higher Education 24th to 17th, while NTU's bigger move was on Times Higher Education, from 54th to 31st, holding roughly steady on QS (13th to 12th). <strong>A system with only two internationally ranked universities pushed both of them further up, rather than adding a third: pick fewer, fund longer.</strong>
</p>

<p style={{ marginTop: 32 }}>
The UK is the clearest test of that stability, because it wasn't just holding steady, it was absorbing a real shock. Its Times Higher Education WUR Top-100 count held at roughly eleven institutions through 2021 to 2024, the same years its universities lost preferential access to Horizon Europe funding after Brexit; full re-association wasn't restored until September 2023. <strong>A four-year disruption to the EU's flagship research-grant programme, and the UK's elite-tier footprint barely moved: deep reputation and alumni networks buy years of buffer against a policy shock that would sink a system with less of either.</strong>
</p>

<strong>The real movement in this decade's data is happening below this cohort, among institutions that weren't elite in 2016 at all.</strong> Isolating the fastest of those newcomers makes the funding pattern explicit.
</p>

<p style={{ marginTop: 32 }}>
<strong>The list of biggest risers is, in effect, a list of national funding programmes.</strong> Five of the top ten are mainland Chinese universities funded under the Double First-Class scheme, launched in 2017 to succeed the older, broader Project 985 and Project 211<sup>‡</sup> with a shortlist of roughly 140 universities and disciplines named for funding aimed explicitly at building "world-class" research capacity. Shanghai Jiao Tong, Zhejiang, Fudan, Nanjing, and the University of Science and Technology of China were already large, established research universities before the policy; the funding built on existing infrastructure rather than creating it from nothing, which is a large part of why their climbs came as quickly as they did. Two more risers are Hong Kong universities (Hong Kong Polytechnic and the Chinese University of Hong Kong) compounding on a separate funding base, the same combination of existing capacity plus new money. Two are Korean universities (Yonsei and KAIST) funded by Brain Korea 21, a graduate-research programme now in its fourth phase since 1999 (the current phase, BK21 FOUR, runs 2020 to 2027), longer-running than the THE rankings themselves; Korea's climb reflects two and a half decades of compounding investment rather than a single policy launch, which is why Korean risers read as steadier, longer climbs rather than one sharp jump. The remaining risers are Berlin's Charité, funded by Germany's Excellence Strategy, and the University of Technology Sydney, whose climb from rank 196 to rank 145 is real but far more modest than the Chinese, Hong Kong or Korean entries.
</p>

<p style={{ marginTop: 32 }}>
Not all of these risers are STEM specialists. A handful are explicitly technical institutes (Hong Kong Polytechnic, the University of Science and Technology of China, KAIST, KTH Royal Institute of Technology) or a specialist medical university (Charité Berlin), but most are comprehensive research universities with strengths well beyond engineering and the hard sciences. Fudan, for instance, is particularly known for the humanities and social sciences within China, not for being a STEM specialist. The funding programmes behind these climbs target research output broadly, not STEM alone.
</p>


<p style={{ marginTop: 32 }}>
University of Technology Sydney's presence here is worth reading carefully. Its climb is one university's success story inside a national system that this report treats elsewhere as a cautionary tale, not a funding success story. Australia's structural exposure is real: its universities lean heavily on international-student revenue, and that funding model took the sharpest hit to any major host country's international-outlook score during the 2020-to-2022 border closures. The Australian National University alone fell from THE rank 50 in 2020 to 73 by 2025. UTS's climb reflects a deliberate strategy, not immunity from the shock: a named institutional plan (UTS 2027) targeted research quality and industry ties specifically, and as a university only since 1988, it had far more room to grow from a lower base than an already-elite ANU had room to fall. <strong>One institution's rise and a system's fragility can both be true at once.</strong>
</p>

<p style={{ marginTop: 32 }}>
<strong>One pattern holds across the entire risers list: universities that climbed are universities whose governments committed to sustained research funding, and the climb showed up years after the funding started.</strong> No riser in this list got there without that combination: sustained government funding, a mechanism in place to hold institutions accountable, and a university able to convert it into research output. Exactly how many years varies: China's climbs mostly show up within two to four years of the 2017 launch, faster than the five-to-seven-year lag this section describes elsewhere, likely because Double First-Class built on universities that already had the infrastructure to convert money into output quickly. Korea's climb, compounding over two and a half decades, does not map onto a single lag window at all.
</p>

<p style={{ marginTop: 32 }}>
That funding-to-climb mechanism is the substance behind this section's regional framing: <strong>national commitment eventually shows up in the rankings, consistently, regardless of region</strong>, though how long it takes depends on how much research infrastructure a system already had to build on. Section 03, GCC Spotlight, works through the same mechanism for the Gulf specifically, where the lag runs closer to five to seven years.
</p>

<h3 style={{ display: 'flex', alignItems: 'center', gap: 10, marginTop: 40 }}><Flag country="United Arab Emirates" width={24} /> United Arab Emirates</h3>

<p>
In the UAE, Khalifa moved from the QS 401-410 band in 2017<sup>‡</sup> to integer rank 177 in 2026, <strong>a roughly 228-place gain and the largest decade-long rise of any Gulf university in either ranking</strong>. On Times Higher Education, Khalifa entered the rankings in 2018 in the 301-350 band and now sits in the 201-250 band, just outside the integer-ranked tier. Abu Dhabi University moved faster still on Times Higher Education: entering the WUR Top 500 in 2023 already in the 301-350 band, it reached an outright rank of 191 by 2025 (<strong>the only UAE institution to escape THE's banding entirely this decade</strong>) before settling back into the 201-250 tier alongside Khalifa in 2026.
</p>

<p>
The structural explanation is the 2017 consolidation that brought Khalifa University, the Petroleum Institute and the Masdar Institute together into a single research-intensive flagship. The pattern in the underlying scores is what makes the case instructive: <strong>Khalifa's research-output scores moved more than its reputation scores. The gain came from doing more research, not from being better known for it, and reputation typically catches up a few years later.</strong>
</p>

<p>
There's no single published roadmap behind the climb, but the intent behind it is well-documented. Khalifa's Board of Trustees is appointed by decree from Abu Dhabi's Executive Council and stacked with senior officials from Mubadala, ADNOC, ADQ and the UAE Space Agency, rather than run as a conventional university board. The 2017 merger decree itself set only a general aspiration ("place the unified university among the best in the world"), not a numbered target like Saudi Arabia's "at least five in the global Top 200." And the university's leadership has been explicit about intent: on entering the QS Top 150, President Ebrahim Al Hajri said the achievement "reflects a deliberate choice to ensure the discoveries made in our laboratories reach scientists, industries, and institutions around the world."
</p>

<p>
Abu Dhabi University's rise runs on a different engine. Chancellor Ghassan Aouad, previously president of Applied Science University in Bahrain for eight years, took over in January 2023 (the same year the university first entered the Times Higher Education WUR Top 500), and pushed research output, citations and industry engagement broadly rather than through a single merger. The university's own figures point to what that push actually looked like: cumulative Scopus-indexed publications passed 4,000 by April 2025 and 5,000 by February 2026; eight new degree programmes launched for the 2024-25 academic year, including a PhD in Law; and faculty hiring expanded in strategic fields such as engineering and computer science, where several ADU researchers, including one with 270 Scopus papers and over 4,000 citations, now rank among the world's top 2 percent of cited researchers. <strong>Abu Dhabi University now ranks first in the UAE for citations per paper</strong>, and its International Outlook score, built on international faculty and student recruitment, ranks 13th globally. It is a second UAE institution running a research-led strategy, reached without Khalifa's consolidation story.
</p>

<p>
The model is what makes the case worth attention. The UAE merged two specialist institutions, the Petroleum Institute and the Masdar Institute, into Khalifa University, then funded the consolidated flagship as a Vision 2071 priority. This is the Singapore approach (one or two world-class flagships, sustained over decades) at a one-flagship scale.
</p>

<h3 style={{ display: 'flex', alignItems: 'center', gap: 10 }}><Flag country="Saudi Arabia" width={24} /> Saudi Arabia</h3>

<p>
Saudi Arabia took a different route to a bigger outcome. Vision 2030 set an explicit, time-bound target ("at least five institutions in the global Top 200") and funded a broader base of universities rather than a single flagship: <strong>nine Saudi institutions now sit in the Times Higher Education WUR Top 500, up from one in 2016</strong>, and five in the QS WUR Top 500, up from three in 2017. King Fahd University of Petroleum and Minerals leads the cohort at QS 67, but the growth is spread across public universities in Riyadh, Jeddah and the Eastern Province, not concentrated in one campus.
</p>

<p>
King Fahd University of Petroleum and Minerals's own climb to QS 67 has a specific, well-documented origin. Vision 2030 converted it from a government institution into an independent, non-profit entity in 2020, and Dr. Muhammad Al-Saggaf, its first president since that independence, took over the same year after leading KAPSARC, an independent nonprofit energy-research institute, rather than coming up through a conventional university system. Under his leadership King Fahd University of Petroleum and Minerals built out 20 new specialised research centres, added 43 new undergraduate concentrations, and grew its postdoctoral researcher base roughly tenfold toward a stated target of 1,500. The payoff took a few years to show: its QS rank barely moved through 2023, then climbed from 160 to 101 in 2025 and to 67 in 2026, <strong>making it the first Arab university to break into the QS WUR Top 100</strong>.
</p>

<p>
One absence from this cohort is KAUST: both QS and Times Higher Education require an institution to teach at undergraduate as well as postgraduate level to qualify for their World University Rankings, and KAUST, built in 2009 as a graduate-only research university, does not meet that bar. The same graduate-only design shows up instead in the rankings that don't carry that eligibility rule: <strong>KAUST has placed first in Times Higher Education's Arab University Rankings for three consecutive years (2023-2025)</strong>, though it is not the top Saudi entry in QS's equivalent regional ranking, where King Fahd University of Petroleum and Minerals holds first place.
</p>

<p>
That broad base isn't uniformly durable, though, and King Abdulaziz University is the cautionary case inside it. King Abdulaziz reached an outright Times Higher Education rank of 101 in 2023 (within reach of the global WUR Top 100), then fell to the 251-300 band in 2024 and the 351-400 band by 2025, where it remains in 2026. Part of that rise was built on King Abdulaziz University incentivizing prominent international researchers to add it as a secondary affiliation.<sup>§</sup> This was part of a pattern reported on Saudi universities generally since the early 2010s that inflated highly-cited-researcher counts and citation-impact scores without a matching gain in research capacity on the ground. When Times Higher Education cut the weight of raw citation impact from 30 percent to 15 percent of the overall score in its 2024 methodology overhaul, King Abdulaziz's rank fell with it. King Fahd's climb held up across the same rule change. <strong>A rank built on research and industry ties survives a methodology rewrite; a rank built on citation engineering does not.</strong>
</p>

<h3 style={{ display: 'flex', alignItems: 'center', gap: 10 }}><Flag country="Qatar" width={24} /> Qatar</h3>

<p>
Qatar's footprint tells the opposite story: small, and largely unchanged. But two structural factors make Qatar's footprint look smaller than it really is. Qatar's population is under three million, the smallest base of any GCC system in this report by a wide margin, and its most visible higher-education investment doesn't register in a country count at all: the Education City branch campuses of Georgetown, Northwestern, Cornell, Texas A&M<sup>†</sup>, Carnegie Mellon, Virginia Commonwealth University and HEC Paris are ranked under their home institutions, not as Qatari entries. </p>

<p style={{ marginTop: 8, marginBottom: 8 }}><strong>Two mechanisms explain almost every rank-gap surprise.</strong></p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Reputation lags reality, on both sides.</strong> QS asks tens of thousands of academics and employers what they think of universities, 45% of its score. THE runs its own academic reputation survey too, for 33% of its score. Either way, reputation views update slowly: a university that improved five years ago is still being judged on its older work, on either list. A university that slipped five years ago still benefits from its old reputation.</li>
  <li><strong>Research output moves faster.</strong> A university that publishes more, and is cited more, climbs the research-output side of the rankings in three to five years. Times Higher Education rewards this more than QS does (30% of the THE score sits in purely bibliometric Research Quality indicators, against 20% in QS's Citations per Faculty).</li>
  <li><strong>Together, they sort universities into two types.</strong> A Chinese research university that rose on citation output, like the University of Science and Technology of China (QS 132, THE 51), typically ranks higher on Times Higher Education: the research-speed mechanism. A long-established American university with a deep reputation footprint, like Cornell (QS 16, THE 18), typically ranks higher on QS: the reputation-lag mechanism.</li>
  <li><strong>The same two mechanisms explain wider gaps too.</strong> University of California, Santa Cruz (QS 458, THE 181) runs the opposite way: a public research campus with a research profile that shows up well across THE's broader pillar structure, but without the global reputation-survey visibility or the strong faculty-to-student ratio that QS's heavier 55% reputation-plus-ratio weighting rewards. Neither ranking is wrong; they are weighing different things.</li>

  kicker="Outlook to 2030"
  title="Five forces will reshape the league tables before 2030."

<p className="lead">
The decade's clearest lesson is that rankings move slowest at the very top, fastest at the bottom of the WUR Top 500, and most discontinuously when the publishers change their rules. The next five years will be shaped by five forces, none of them in the universities' direct control.
</p>

<h3>AI-augmented research is the single largest variable, and it cuts both ways.</h3>
<p>
Adoption is already large and undisclosed: <strong>a 2025 study found that roughly 14 percent of biomedical abstracts indexed on PubMed in 2024 carried the textual signatures of large-language-model writing</strong>, and disclosure rates lag far behind actual use.<sup>†</sup> Universities that institutionalise AI tools in their research operations (for literature review, hypothesis generation, experimental design, and analysis) will see their citation scores compound faster than universities that do not. The differential will not be uniform: it will favour large, integrated research universities with the data infrastructure to operationalise AI at scale, and work against smaller specialist institutions that lack the supporting systems. <strong>Expect a sharper separation between the global Top 30 and the rest of the WUR Top 100 by 2030.</strong>
</p>

<p style={{ marginTop: 24 }}>
So far, that is AI reviewing, assisting and speeding up research that humans are still doing. The more dramatic shift is AI starting to produce the research itself, and the clearest example sits at the frontier of pure mathematics. <strong>The Euler and Navier-Stokes equations, which describe how fluids flow, are among the oldest unsolved problems in mathematical physics</strong>: Euler wrote his version down in 1757, and the Navier-Stokes equations reached their modern form by 1845. Whether solutions to either can spontaneously "blow up," developing a singularity in finite time, has been an open question for close to two centuries; proving it for the Navier-Stokes case has carried a $1 million prize from the Clay Mathematics Institute since 2000. In September 2026, OpenAI said an internal model had itself produced a proof that the three-dimensional Navier-Stokes equations can develop such a singularity, days after two mathematicians working partly with Anthropic's models claimed a related result on the simpler Euler equations. Neither claim has been peer-reviewed, and the two teams are disputing whether OpenAI's work drew on leaked details of the other's; both the speed of the claims and the dispute over them are what AI-generated research now looks like at the frontier, not merely AI-assisted research.
</p>

<p style={{ marginTop: 24 }}>
<strong>The same tools cut the other way, too.</strong> In plain terms: generative AI has made it cheap to mass-produce low-quality research papers, and journals are now catching and retracting roughly ten times as many of them as they did a decade ago.
</p>

<h3>Geopolitical fragmentation is a real and growing risk.</h3>
<p>
<strong>Russia is the only major country in our analysis whose citation and research-quality score has moved negatively over the decade</strong>, consistent with its isolation from Western research networks following 2022. A similar but slower decoupling between the United States and China would have far larger effects, because the citation networks involved are larger. Cross-border research collaboration is a tailwind for many of the universities in the current WUR Top 100; if it becomes a constraint, the geographic distribution of the elite tier will narrow. Neutral-ground research hosts, including the UAE, would become relative beneficiaries.
</p>

<h3>Demographics will tighten Western higher-education margins.</h3>
<p>
<strong>The shrinking 18-year-old populations of North America, Western Europe, and East Asia</strong> (notably South Korea and Japan) will tighten margins at universities that depend on tuition revenue. The demographic dividend in India, Southeast Asia, and Sub-Saharan Africa is real, but <strong>will not produce new WUR Top-500 universities for at least 15 years</strong>: new universities take that long to build the research depth, reputation footprint, and faculty base the rankings require. The compositional shifts of the next decade will continue to flow through existing institutions, not new ones.
</p>

<h3>The single "world ranking" is fragmenting into a portfolio of rankings.</h3>
<p>
QS and Times Higher Education already run regional and specialist products, alongside their flagship World University Rankings, with different eligibility rules and different winners. Section 03 already shows what this looks like in practice: <strong>KAUST cannot appear in either flagship table at all</strong> (both require an undergraduate programme, which KAUST does not have), yet it has topped Times Higher Education's Arab University Rankings for three consecutive years, and King Fahd University of Petroleum and Minerals, not KAUST, leads the equivalent QS regional table. <strong>A university's rank increasingly depends on which list is being asked, not just where it stands. By 2030, "how is this university ranked" will need a follow-up question: in which ranking, measuring what.</strong>
</p>

<h3>A fifth force: the publishers are not done rewriting the rules.</h3>
<p>
This last one is informed speculation rather than a documented fact like the other four, but it is speculation grounded in this decade's own pattern. <strong>Neither publisher has gone long without a material methodology change</strong>: QS added Sustainability and International Research Network as new indicators in 2023 and introduced International Student Diversity in 2025; Times Higher Education shipped the WUR 3.0 rewrite in 2024. Times Higher Education's 2024 rewrite, in particular, followed exactly the kind of citation-engineering this report documents at King Abdulaziz University in Section 03, and that is exactly the kind of gaming AI now makes cheaper and easier to scale. <strong>Our best guess is that the next material rule change targets research-integrity screening directly</strong>: something that discounts citations tied to retracted papers, flags AI-generated or paper-mill output before it is folded into a citations score, or requires disclosed AI use in the underlying publications, rather than catching it years later through retractions. We could be wrong about the specific mechanism. What this decade's data makes hard to bet against is the direction: <strong>another rewrite is coming, and research integrity, not teaching quality or reputation, is the most exposed part of either methodology today.</strong> Any university or funder making a five-year ranking strategy that does not include "the rules may change again" as a planning assumption is making a fragile bet.
</p>

<ArticleSection id="implications" variant="alt">

<SectionHeader
  number="06"
  kicker="Implications"
  title="Fund with intent: outcomes don't happen by accident."
/>

<p className="lead">
This decade's data speaks most directly to the governments and sovereign funders who decide where the money goes. What follows is built on the same two or three mechanisms that run through the rest of this report: reputation lag, funding-to-climb lag, and how much a system concentrates its effort versus spreads it thin.
</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Be clear about what you want from rankings before you fund for them.</strong> Rankings reward research quality, internationalisation and employment outcomes; QS weights reputation surveys and research output most heavily, while THE leans harder into research quality and teaching. They do not measure teaching quality, social mobility, or regional impact. Funding institutions to climb the rankings means funding the things rankings measure, not institutional quality overall.</li>
  <li><strong>Fund a selective number of fields or institutions on ten-year cycles, rather than three-year budgets, and fund what the rankings actually reward:</strong> research output, citations, international research networks, and employment outcomes, not names on buildings. These are the specific, measurable inputs behind every score; a building doesn't move a citations-per-faculty number, but a decade of sustained faculty hiring in a chosen field does.</li>
  <li><strong>Set a numbered, time-bound target, and report against it annually.</strong> Saudi Arabia did this. "At least five institutions in the Top 200 by 2030" is the kind of target that focuses ministerial effort and creates accountability. It paid off faster than the deadline required, too: King Fahd University of Petroleum and Minerals became the first Arab university to break into the QS Top 100 years ahead of 2030.</li>
  <li><strong>Building from scratch and funding an existing university run on different timelines; pick knowing which one you're signing up for.</strong> Building a new university from nothing (KAUST, MBZUAI) takes more than 15 years to produce Top-200 standing; funding an existing university harder (China's Double First-Class, Khalifa after 2017) shows up in five to seven years. Both work, but a new minister or board changing strategy after three years is one of the most common and costly mistakes in this domain. Patience doesn't have to mean no oversight, though: Double First-Class pairs its long funding cycles with real accountability, publicly warning or revoking underperforming disciplines at each five-year review rather than funding indefinitely with no check on results.</li>
  <li><strong>Treat international-student visa policy as research policy.</strong> International graduates who stay co-author papers that compound into citation scores over five to ten years, and both major ranking systems reward this directly: it feeds QS's international-network indicators and THE's International Outlook pillar. The UAE Golden Visa, the UK Graduate Route, the German Blue Card and the Singapore TechPass are all, in effect, citation-score instruments, and exactly the kind of open channel geopolitical fragmentation would close first.</li>
  <li><strong>A hosted branch campus almost never earns a rank of its own, so judge it by what it does for your other universities, not by a climb it can't make.</strong> Its researchers co-author with your home-grown institutions, and that collaboration lifts those universities' international-network and co-authorship scores, whether or not the branch campus itself ever appears in a league table. Qatar's Education City model, which hosts branch campuses of Georgetown, Northwestern, Cornell and others, is the clearest example of this in practice: none of those campuses shows up as a separate Qatari entry in either ranking, yet the model still does real work for the country's research profile.</li>
</ul>

<p style={{ marginTop: 32 }}>
None of this changes what already happened. It only changes what to do next.
</p>
