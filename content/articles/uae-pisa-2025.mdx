<ArticleSection id="pisa">

<SectionHeader
  number="01"
  kicker="PISA"
  title="A test of real-world reasoning"
/>

- The OECD's PISA tests 15-year-olds in reading, mathematics and science, on how well they apply knowledge to real-life problems
  - The test is computer-based and adaptive, set by age rather than grade, and done in local language (English or Arabic in UAE)
  - PISA has run every 3 years since 2000; it will next shift to a 4-year cycle
  - The OECD reports mean scores by subject only; overall country rankings are produced by third parties

</ArticleSection>

<ArticleSection id="global">

<SectionHeader
  number="02"
  kicker="A decade of global decline"
  title="Reading is pulling down every subject"
/>

- PISA 2025 recorded the lowest OECD averages ever: 482 in science, 461 in reading and 463 in mathematics
  - Since 2015 the OECD average has lost 28 points in reading and 22 in mathematics, equivalent to more than a year of learning
  - Reading is at the core of the decline. Students read less and are ~2x as likely to give quick, wrong answers as in 2018. As every PISA question is text, weaker reading also pulls down mathematics and science
- Countries are moving in different directions
  - Former leaders are slipping, e.g., Finland, once the reading benchmark, has declined in all three subjects, Australia's mathematics is 46 points below 2003, and US scores are at record lows
  - Europe saw the sharpest recent falls: between 2022 and 2025, e.g., reading dropped 23 points in Spain, 18 in France and 15 in Germany
  - East Asian systems, led by China, Singapore, Macao, and Japan, remain on top, with Estonia and the United Kingdom in the top ten
  - Countries who have been improving consistently started from a low base: since 2006 Qatar has gained 85 points in science, Colombia has gained 26 points, and Türkiye almost 70 in a decade
  - Only eight countries, UAE among them, improved in all three subjects between 2022 and 2025

</ArticleSection>

<ArticleSection id="uae">

<SectionHeader
  number="03"
  kicker="UAE"
  title="Breaking a decade of stagnation"
/>

- The UAE was one of only eight countries to improve in all three subjects versus 2022 results (+26 points in science, +21 in mathematics and +15 in reading)
  - 2012 to 2022 was a decade without progress for UAE. Science scores fell from 448 to 432 and reading from 442 to 417; mathematics stayed at around 430
  - In 2025 science rose by 26 points, mathematics by 21 and reading by 15, taking science and mathematics to their highest level since 2012
- At the current pace, UAE would pass today's OECD average in mathematics and science by 2029; reading would remain 13 points short
- Dubai shows what parts of the system already achieve. 506 points in science, 496 in mathematics and 484 in reading, it scores around 50 points above the UAE average and above the OECD average in all subjects

<h3>Equity: large gaps between students, but they are narrowing from the bottom</h3>

- In the UAE, a student's family background has limited influence on their score: it explains only 3.3% of the variation in results, against 11.6% across the OECD; the bigger divide is between expatriate students, that score 94 points above non-expatriates
- Disadvantaged students gained 40 points in science (vs. 19 of advantaged), and low performers fell by 10 p.p.

<h3>Improvement does not seem to be driven by AI</h3>

- The Ministry of Education partly attributed the results to AI and digital readiness, but the national AI curriculum started in government schools only in 2025-26 and in private schools in September 2026, after students took the test
- Additionally, 64% of UAE students use AI weekly to learn (OECD average is 46%), yet PISA finds students who hand schoolwork to AI score lower, not higher

<h3>Drivers of improvement: accountability and familiarity with the test</h3>

- PISA data alone makes it difficult to pinpoint what drove the UAE gains. However, they most likely reflect accountability pressure built up over a decade
  - Targets: The 2014 National Agenda set a top 20 PISA target. Dubai's Education 33 Strategy (2024) aims for a top 10 place for its private schools, and ADEK reportedly shares that ambition
  - Support: ADEK ran workshops for school leads on the PISA framework and sample questions; SPEA trained all Sharjah schools and gave every student an online practice platform
  - Inspection: Schools are rated from Outstanding to Weak under a single national framework. PISA is not named in it, but since 2022 inspectors were encouraged to weigh PISA results heavily
  - Consequences: In Dubai, a school that improves its rating can raise fees by 2x the cost index, while one that slips cannot raise them. As most UAE schools are private, PISA results became linked to revenue
- Schools responded by adding PISA to their priorities. They identified 4 challenges and tackled them:
  - Purpose: before 2022, PISA had no consequences for schools, and many teachers did not see its value. Schools trained teachers on what PISA tests are and why it matters
  - Question style: students used to exams that test recall struggled with PISA's long, real-life problems. Schools introduced regular practice of PISA-style questions
  - Language: most students sit the test (done in English or Arabic) in a second language (in 2022, 57% spoke a different language at home). Practice also built the vocabulary PISA questions rely on
  - Format: many students had little experience of on-screen, adaptive tests. Online practice, such as SPEA's platform in Sharjah, helped close that gap
- Much of the 2025 gain therefore came from familiarity with the test, which tends to be a one-off

<ArticleSection id="ai">

<SectionHeader
  number="04"
  kicker="AI in schools"
  title="Useful only when students learn to question it"
/>

- 86% of OECD students already use AI for schoolwork. The impact on PISA results are based on how AI is used
  - Students who hand summarising or drafting to AI score ~20 points lower in science
  - Moderate AI use makes little difference in scores, and students taught AI literacy score slightly higher
  - AI makes reading even more important, yet the reading skills it relies on most, such as judging information and comparing sources, are declining
  - Evidence of the impact of AI on student outcomes is mixed, with experts stating it is too early to know how AI will impact student scores
- Leading systems and schools are setting up policies before scaling up AI usage
  - Estonia trained teachers first, then gave upper-secondary students an AI app designed to guide them to answers rather than supply them
  - China made AI lessons compulsory from age six but stops primary school students from using AI on their own
  - South Korea reversed its rollout of AI-enabled textbooks, after only 30% of schools adopted them
- The UAE is well equipped but untested
  - 82% of UAE students are taught to assess AI-generated information (OECD average is 63%), schools rank first on the OECD index of digital capacity in teaching, and a national K-12 AI curriculum will train 22,000 teachers
  - PISA 2029 makes reading the main subject and adds an optional Media and AI Literacy assessment: the first external test of the curriculum, if the UAE takes part

</ArticleSection>

<ArticleSection id="implications">

<SectionHeader
  number="05"
  kicker="Looking forward"
  title="Implications for the UAE"
/>

- The OECD average is within reach for the UAE; the top 10 is not yet
  - Mathematics and science could reach the OECD average in 2029 if the pace holds; reading will take longer
  - In science, the 20th-ranked system scored 490 and the 10th 509. Dubai (506) is close, so the gap lies mostly in the rest of the system
- The next gains must come from learning, as familiarity gains will not repeat
  - Make reading the priority: 12.8% of UAE reading answers were fast and wrong (OECD: 8.9%)
  - Support the schools furthest behind by pairing penalties with coaching and teacher training
  - Inspect for learning, not test practice, by assessing how students read, reason and write
- Build AI literacy on reading: teach students to question AI output before relying on it, and join PISA 2029's Media and AI Literacy assessment to measure the AI curriculum
